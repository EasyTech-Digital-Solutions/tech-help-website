Smart TV Setup: What Most People Get Wrong

Setting up a new Smart TV sounds like it should take ten minutes — unbox it, connect it to Wi-Fi, sign into a couple of apps, done. But most TVs leave the factory tuned for a brightly lit showroom floor, not your living room, and a handful of default settings quietly work against picture quality, audio, and even the physical remote. Here's what to fix.

1. Disable "Vivid" or "Dynamic" picture mode. This is the single biggest picture-quality mistake. Vivid mode boosts brightness and oversaturates color so the TV stands out under harsh store lighting — it looks garish and unnatural at home. Switch to "Movie," "Cinema," or "Filmmaker" mode instead; it's calibrated much closer to how the content was actually meant to look, with accurate skin tones and proper black levels.

2. Connect to the 5 GHz Wi-Fi band, not 2.4 GHz. Streaming 4K video needs real bandwidth, and the 2.4 GHz band is usually crowded with every other device in the house. If your TV connected to 2.4 GHz automatically during setup, go into network settings and switch it to your 5 GHz network — buffering and quality drops during busy viewing hours often disappear entirely.

3. Use HDMI ARC or eARC for your soundbar, not optical. If you're adding a soundbar or AV receiver, connect it through an HDMI ARC (or eARC, for higher-end setups) port rather than an optical cable. Beyond better audio quality — including Dolby Atmos support that optical can't carry — ARC lets your TV remote control the soundbar's volume directly, so you're not juggling two remotes.

4. Check for a pending software update. TV manufacturers push regular firmware updates that fix app crashes, improve streaming stability, and occasionally unlock new features. It's easy to skip this during initial setup — check Settings → Support/About for a pending update once everything else is connected.

5. Consolidate your streaming devices under one remote. If you're running an Apple TV, Fire Stick, or Chromecast alongside the TV's built-in smart platform, switching between them can get confusing fast. Most modern TVs and streaming devices support HDMI-CEC, which lets a single remote control everything — turning on the TV, switching inputs, and adjusting volume, all without picking up a second remote.
