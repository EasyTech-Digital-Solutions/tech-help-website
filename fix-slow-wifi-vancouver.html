How to Fix Slow Wi-Fi at Home

If your Wi-Fi feels slower than it should — buffering video calls, pages that won't load, or a signal that drops the moment you walk into another room — the cause is almost always one of three things: router placement, channel congestion, or an overloaded device. None of them require new equipment to start diagnosing, and most can be fixed in under twenty minutes.

1. Move your router to a central, elevated location. Wi-Fi signal weakens with distance and is blocked by walls, floors, and metal appliances. A router tucked in a closet or behind the TV is fighting an uphill battle before it even starts. Placing it centrally and up off the floor — on a shelf, not the ground — noticeably improves coverage in most homes.

2. Keep interference sources away from your router. Microwaves, cordless phones, and baby monitors all operate in the same 2.4 GHz range as older Wi-Fi and can cause real, measurable slowdowns when your router is nearby. If you notice your Wi-Fi drops specifically when the microwave is running, that's your answer.

3. Switch nearby devices to the 5 GHz band. Most modern routers broadcast two networks — 2.4 GHz (longer range, slower, more congested) and 5 GHz (shorter range, much faster). For devices that are close to the router — a laptop in the same room, a streaming stick — log into your router settings and connect them to the 5 GHz network instead. It's often the single biggest speed improvement available for free.

4. Consider mesh Wi-Fi for larger homes. If you're in a larger home, a townhouse, or a multi-level house in Coquitlam or Burnaby, a single router — no matter how good — often can't cover the whole space evenly. A mesh system like Google Nest Wifi or Eero uses multiple small units working together, and is far more effective than a router-plus-extender setup, which frequently creates its own dead zones and connection drops.

5. Restart your router regularly. Routers accumulate cached connections and memory clutter over weeks of continuous uptime. A simple restart — once a week is plenty — clears this out and often produces a noticeable, if temporary, speed boost.

When it's not your Wi-Fi at all. If you've tried all of the above and speeds are still poor, the problem is often upstream — your internet service plan, your modem, or an issue with your ISP. A quick way to check: run a speed test with a laptop plugged directly into the modem via Ethernet. If speeds are still slow with no Wi-Fi involved at all, the issue isn't your router.
